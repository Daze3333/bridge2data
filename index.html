    Understanding P-values

    A small concept that appears everywhere in postgraduate research.

    1 · What is it?

    A p-value is a “how surprising is this?” number.

    It helps you judge whether the pattern in your sample could reasonably have appeared by chance. A small p-value means the result would be unusual if there were truly no effect or difference.

    2 · Why do I need it?

    It helps you interpret research results.

    Professors and journal articles often use p-values when discussing hypotheses. You may need to report one after a t-test, ANOVA, correlation or regression.

    3 · Simple example

    Two advertisements, one question.

    A marketing student compares purchase intention after Advertisement A and Advertisement B. The test returns p = 0.03.

    Using a common threshold of 0.05, the result is statistically significant. The observed difference would be relatively unusual if the ads truly performed the same.

    4 · Try it yourself

    Your survey test returns p = 0.12. Is it significant at the 0.05 level?

    Think about whether 0.12 is smaller or larger than 0.05.

    No. Because 0.12 is larger than 0.05, you would not call this result statistically significant at the 0.05 level. This does not prove there is no effect—it means the study did not provide strong enough evidence under this rule.
